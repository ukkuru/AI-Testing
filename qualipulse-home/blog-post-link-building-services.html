Most pitches for link building services sound the same. A set number of links a month, a minimum authority score, a dashboard. For a SaaS company, that is usually the wrong thing to buy. A monthly link building service should earn links from sites your buyers read, point them at pages that matter to revenue, and stay well clear of anything Google calls link spam.

This guide covers what a good retainer includes, how to judge link quality, the red flags, enterprise link building, how to compare the best link building companies, and how to measure the work.

What a Monthly Link Building Service Actually Does

Strip away the sales language and a monthly link building service does three jobs.

  • It finds reasons for other sites to link to you. That means research, data, tools, expert quotes and pages that are more useful than what already exists on a topic.
  • It gets those reasons in front of the right people. Editors, writers, community owners and partners who already cover your category.
  • It reports what happened. Which links went live, on which sites, pointing to which pages, and what changed for those pages afterwards.

Most vendors skip the first job. Outreach without anything worth linking to turns into begging, and begging at scale turns into paying.

For software companies, your buyers often read a narrow set of sources: engineering blogs, practitioner newsletters, community forums, integration partner docs and a few trade publications. A link from one of those beats a link from a big general site whose readers will never buy.

What a Good Link Building Retainer Should Include

Here is how we would read a retainer proposal, line by line.

Deliverable What good looks like Red flag
Prospect researchA named list of sites your buyers read, with the reason each one fits and the page you want linkedA spreadsheet sorted only by authority score, with no mention of audience
Linkable assetsOriginal research, practitioner guides, free tools or templates built so writers have something to citeNo content work at all, only outreach to existing sales pages
Digital PRExpert commentary, data stories and founder quotes pitched to journalists who cover your categoryPress releases blasted to distribution networks for the links
Resource and broken link outreachFinding dead links and outdated resource pages on relevant sites, then offering a page that genuinely replaces themGeneric templates sent to thousands of unrelated sites
Unlinked brand mentionsMonitoring where your product or people are named without a link, and asking politely for oneNever checked, even though it is often the easiest win
Guest contributionsBylined articles on sites your buyers read, written by someone who knows the subject, with a link only where it helps the reader"Guest posts" on sites that publish anything for a fee, with keyword-stuffed anchor text
ReportingEvery live link listed with the site, target page, anchor and link attributes, plus movement on the target pagesA link count and an average authority score, with no URLs you can check

Notice what is missing from the good column: a guaranteed number. Editorial links depend on editors saying yes. A vendor who promises an exact count of quality links every month is usually buying them somewhere.

How to Judge Link Quality

You do not need a paid tool to judge most links. Open the page and ask five questions.

Is the site relevant to your buyers?

A QA platform earning a link from a testing community makes sense. The same platform on a travel blog does not, whatever the authority score says.

Does the site have real readers?

Look for signs of life: recent posts, comments, named authors with real profiles, a newsletter. A site with plenty of links but no readers is a warning sign.

Was the link placed editorially?

An editorial link is one a writer chose because it helps their reader. It sits in the body of an article with natural anchor wording. Links stuffed into footers, sidebars or a block of unrelated links at the end of a post are not editorial.

Is it a link farm?

Some sites exist to sell placements. Tells: every article links out to unrelated businesses, topics jump from crypto to dentistry to SaaS in one week, and there is a "write for us" page with a price list.

Dofollow or nofollow?

Let us be honest about this one. "Dofollow" is not a real attribute. It is shorthand for a normal link with no qualifier. Google asks sites to mark paid links with rel="sponsored", user-generated links like forum posts with rel="ugc", and other links they do not want to vouch for with rel="nofollow". Google's guide to qualifying outbound links says links marked with these values will generally not be followed.

So a normal editorial link usually carries more SEO value. That does not make nofollow links worthless. A nofollow mention in a well-read forum thread can still send real buyers to your site. Judge a link by who will see it first, and by its attributes second.

Red Flags: Bought Links, PBNs and Guaranteed Packages

Google is direct about this. Its spam policies define link spam as creating links to or from a site primarily to manipulate search rankings. The examples it lists include buying or selling links for ranking purposes, exchanging goods or services for links, excessive link exchanges, using automated programs or services to create links, advertorials where payment is received for articles with links that pass ranking credit, and links with keyword-targeted anchor text in guest posts or press releases distributed on other sites. The same policy says such links are not a violation when they are qualified with rel="nofollow" or rel="sponsored".

So a properly marked sponsored placement is allowed, but it is marked precisely so it does not pass ranking credit. A vendor selling paid links to lift your rankings is selling what the policy describes.

Walk away from any of these:

  • Paid link networks. Groups of sites that sell placements to anyone who pays.
  • Private blog networks (PBNs). Sites built or bought to link to client sites. They exist to manipulate rankings, which is the exact definition of link spam above.
  • "DA 50+ guaranteed" packages. Domain Authority is a score made by Moz, not Google. Moz itself says Domain Authority is not a Google ranking factor and has no effect on search results. A guarantee built around a third-party score tells you what the vendor is selling to: the report, not the search engine.
  • Link selling marketplaces. Catalogues where you pick a site, pay and get a "guest post" with a link. This is buying links with extra steps.

The risk is real. Google's spam policies page says these tactics can lead to a page or a whole site ranking lower or being left out of results.

Enterprise Link Building Services: What Changes at Scale

Enterprise link building is not the same job with a bigger number. When a software company has several product lines, regional sites and a brand that journalists already write about, the work changes shape.

  • Multiple product lines. Each product has its own buyers, publications and target pages. One shared outreach list sends links to whatever page is easiest.
  • Brand mentions at scale. Large brands get named constantly without a link. A steady process to find and reclaim those mentions often beats new outreach for effort spent.
  • Legal and PR approvals. Quotes, data and bylines need review. A good partner plans approval time into the calendar and keeps a library of approved quotes and facts.
  • Internal linking at scale. Pages that earn external links should pass readers on to product pages through clear internal links. That is often the cheapest win on the list.
  • Clean history. Big sites often carry years of old agency work. Enterprise link building services should audit that before adding more.

How to Compare the Best Link Building Companies

We are not going to rank vendors. Lists of the best link building companies are easy to write and hard to trust, because the vendor that fits a seed-stage dev tool rarely fits a public software company. Ask every vendor the same questions instead.

  1. Can you show me ten links you earned in the last six months, with live URLs?
  2. How do you choose prospect sites? What role does audience relevance play?
  3. Do you ever pay for placements, directly or through a partner? If so, how are those links marked?
  4. Who creates the linkable assets, and does anyone on the team understand our product category?
  5. Which of our pages will you build links to, and why those pages?
  6. What anchor text approach do you use?
  7. What does the monthly report include? Will we see every URL?
  8. What happens to the links if we stop working together?
  9. How will you measure success beyond the number of links?

On pricing, you will usually see three models: per link, a monthly retainer, or hourly. Per-link pricing pushes vendors toward volume and toward sites that say yes for money. A retainer pays for effort, assets and outreach, which suits editorial work. Hourly suits audits and coaching. Whatever the model, ask what the money buys beyond links.

In House vs Agency Link Building

Neither is automatically better. The honest answer depends on what you already have.

In house works when you have people with something to say: founders who speak at conferences, engineers who write well, product data nobody else has.

An agency works when the expertise exists but nobody has time to turn it into research, articles and pitches. Your people show up for interviews and reviews, and the agency does the rest.

How to Measure a Link Building Service

Raw link counts are the easiest number to report and the least useful. Ten links from relevant sites can do more than a hundred from sites nobody reads. Track these instead:

  • Referring domains from relevant sites. Count unique sites your buyers read, not total links. Several links from one site are mostly one vote.
  • Rankings for the target pages. Links were pointed at specific pages for a reason. Watch those pages in Search Console for the queries that matter.
  • Referral traffic. In your analytics tool, check visits from each earned link. A link that sends readers is working even if it never moves a ranking.
  • Pipeline. Follow those visits to demo requests, trials and opportunities in your CRM. Review it quarterly.
  • Link survival. Check that earned links are still live a few months later.

Link Building Services Checklist

  • Does the proposal name the sites and audiences it will target, not only an authority score?
  • Does it include linkable assets, not just outreach?
  • Has the vendor confirmed in writing that it does not buy links, use PBNs or link marketplaces?
  • Are paid or sponsored placements, if any, marked with rel="sponsored"?
  • Have you seen live examples of recent links?
  • Will every live link be reported with URL, anchor and attributes?
  • Are you measuring relevant referring domains, target page rankings, referral traffic and pipeline?
  • For enterprise teams, is there a plan for approvals, brand mentions and internal links?

Frequently Asked Questions

What does a monthly link building service include?

Prospect research on sites your buyers read, linkable assets such as original research or free tools, digital PR, broken link outreach, reclaiming unlinked brand mentions, relevant guest contributions and a report listing every live link with its URL and attributes.

Is buying links against Google's guidelines?

Yes, when the links are meant to pass ranking credit. Google's spam policies list buying or selling links for ranking purposes as link spam. Paid placements are allowed only when they are qualified with rel="sponsored" or rel="nofollow", which tells Google not to treat them as an endorsement.

Are nofollow links worth anything?

Yes, for people rather than rankings. Google says links marked nofollow, sponsored or ugc will generally not be followed, so they usually carry less SEO value. A nofollow mention in a newsletter or forum your buyers read can still send qualified visitors to your site.

How should we measure a link building service?

Track referring domains from relevant sites, rankings for the pages you targeted, referral traffic, pipeline from those visits, and whether links are still live months later. Raw link counts say little about business value.
