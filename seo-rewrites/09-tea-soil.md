### The quick answer for students

If your question paper asks "tea grows in which soil", write this:

* **Soil type:** light, well drained loamy soil or laterite soil, rich in humus and organic matter
* **Soil reaction:** acidic, pH about 4.5 to 5.5
* **Land:** hill slopes or high ground where water drains away freely
* **Climate:** warm and humid, about 20 to 30°C, with 150 to 300 cm of well spread rainfall and no frost

In India, the classic examples are the alluvial and hill soils of Assam, the hill soils of Darjeeling, and the laterite soils of the Nilgiris in the Western Ghats.

## Why tea needs acidic soil

The tea plant, Camellia sinensis, is an acid loving shrub, a bit like azaleas and blueberries. In acidic soil it can take up the nutrients it needs, and it tolerates levels of aluminium that would harm most other crops.

When the pH climbs above about 6, tea struggles. Leaves turn pale and growth slows. On chalky or limestone soils (pH 7 and above), tea usually fails outright. That is why you rarely see tea estates on calcareous land.

Research bodies such as the Tocklai Tea Research Institute in Assam describe ideal tea land as high, well drained soil with good depth, an acidic pH of 4.5 to 5.5, and more than 2% organic matter. The Tea Board of India uses the same 4.5 to 5.5 range as its standard.

### Tea soil pH at a glance

| Soil pH | What happens to tea | What growers do |
|-|-|-|
| Below 4.0 | Too acidic; some nutrients become hard to absorb | Light dressing of dolomite to raise pH slightly |
| 4.5 to 5.5 | Ideal range for healthy growth and good leaf | Maintain with organic matter and balanced feeding |
| 5.5 to 6.0 | Still workable, growth starts to slow | Sulphur or acid forming fertilisers to lower pH |
| Above 6.0 | Poor growth, yellowing leaves | Not recommended for tea planting |

## Which soil type is best for tea cultivation?

pH tells you about chemistry. Soil type tells you about structure, drainage and depth, and tea cares about all three.

### Loamy soil

A deep loam with a good share of sand and organic matter is close to perfect. It drains well but holds enough moisture between rains. Roots can spread, and tea roots go deep, often more than a metre on good land.

### Laterite soil

Laterite soils form in hot, wet regions where heavy rain washes out bases like calcium, leaving soil that is naturally acidic and rich in iron and aluminium oxides. That iron gives them their red colour. Laterite soil is low in fertility on its own, but with manure and fertiliser it suits plantation crops such as tea, coffee and cashew. This is why it shows up in Indian school textbooks as a tea soil.

### Alluvial soil

Alluvial soil is laid down by rivers. In the Brahmaputra valley of Assam, older, well drained alluvial terraces support huge tea estates. The catch is drainage: low lying alluvial land that floods or stays soggy is poor for tea, so estates sit on slightly raised ground and use drains.

### Soils tea dislikes

* Heavy clay that stays waterlogged
* Shallow soil over hard rock or a compacted layer
* Chalky, limestone or alkaline soil
* Low ground where the water table sits close to the surface (Tocklai advises at least 90 cm down)

## Tea growing regions and their soils

### Tea soils in India

| Region | Main soil | Why it works |
|-|-|-|
| Assam (Brahmaputra valley) | Alluvial soils on raised terraces | Deep, fertile and acidic, with heavy monsoon rain |
| Darjeeling (West Bengal hills) | Acidic mountain and forest soils on steep slopes | Slopes drain fast; cool, misty weather slows growth and builds aroma |
| Nilgiris (Tamil Nadu) | Laterite and red loam soils | Acidic, well drained hill land in the Western Ghats |
| Munnar and Wayanad (Kerala) | Laterite and red loamy soils | High rainfall and good slope drainage |
| Dooars and Terai (North Bengal) | Alluvial and foothill soils | Acidic soils with abundant rain |

### Tea soils in other countries

* **Sri Lanka:** acidic red and yellow hill soils in the central highlands, where slope and rainfall give fast drainage.
* **Kenya:** deep, red volcanic soils in the highlands east and west of the Rift Valley. They are acidic, deep and well drained, which is a big part of why Kenya is one of the world's largest tea exporters.
* **China:** red and yellow acidic soils across the southern provinces, often on terraced hillsides.
* **Japan:** acidic soils, many of them derived from volcanic ash, in regions such as Shizuoka and Kagoshima.

The pattern is the same everywhere: acidic, deep, free draining soil, usually on sloping land, with plenty of rain.

## Climate needs for tea

Soil is only half the story. Tea grows where the soil and the climate both fit.

| Factor | What tea needs |
|-|-|
| Temperature | Roughly 20 to 30°C during the growing season; no frost |
| Rainfall | About 150 to 300 cm a year, spread across most months |
| Humidity | High, with frequent mist or cloud in hill areas |
| Altitude | From near sea level (Assam) to over 2,000 m (Darjeeling, Nilgiris, Kenya) |
| Land | Gentle to steep slopes that shed excess water |

Higher, cooler estates grow more slowly. Slower growth usually means more aroma and a lighter, more complex cup, which is part of why hill teas like Darjeeling command higher prices.

## Why drainage matters so much

You will see "well drained" in every answer about tea soil, and for good reason. Tea needs a lot of rain, but its roots rot and suffocate if water sits around them. Shallow or compacted subsoils make it worse: the plant suffers drought in the dry months and waterlogging in the wet ones.

That is why so many estates sit on hillsides. Slopes shed water naturally. On flatter land, such as parts of Assam, estates dig networks of drains to keep the water table low.

## How growers manage tea soil

Good tea soil is not just found, it is looked after. Here is what estates typically do.

### Test the soil regularly

A soil test every year or two tells growers the pH and nutrient levels. That decides how much fertiliser to apply, and whether the soil is drifting out of the ideal pH range.

### Keep the pH in range

Repeated use of ammonium fertilisers slowly acidifies soil. If pH falls well below 4.5, growers apply a small amount of dolomite. If it creeps above 5.5, they use sulphur or acid forming fertilisers to bring it down. Heavy liming is avoided because tea does not like it.

### Build organic matter

Compost, cattle manure, green manure crops and pruning litter left on the ground all add humus. Humus holds moisture, feeds soil life and keeps the topsoil loose.

### Feed with nitrogen, potassium and more

Because the crop is leaves, tea is a heavy user of nitrogen. Potassium, phosphorus, magnesium, sulphur and zinc are also important. The exact mix depends on the soil test.

### Stop erosion on slopes

Hill estates lose topsoil fast if it is left bare. Common fixes:

* Planting along the contour or on terraces
* Mulching with grass or pruned branches
* Cover crops and grasses between young bushes
* Shade trees such as Albizia or silver oak, which also add leaf litter and, in some species, fix nitrogen

### Rest and rehabilitate old land

Before replanting an old tea field, many estates grow a rehabilitation grass, such as Guatemala grass, for a year or more to restore soil structure and organic matter.

## Frequently asked questions

### Which soil is best for tea cultivation?
Deep, well drained, acidic soil with a pH of 4.5 to 5.5 and plenty of humus is best. Light loamy soils and laterite soils, often red from iron, suit tea well. The land should drain freely, which is why most tea is grown on slopes.

### Tea grows in which soil in India?
Tea grows in alluvial soils in the Brahmaputra valley of Assam, in acidic hill and forest soils in Darjeeling, and in laterite and red loamy soils in the Nilgiris and Kerala. All of them are acidic and well drained.

### What is the ideal soil pH for tea?
Between 4.5 and 5.5. Tea struggles above pH 6 and usually fails on chalky or alkaline soils.

### Why does tea need well drained soil?
Tea needs heavy rain, but its roots rot if water collects around them. Well drained soil on sloping land lets the rain pass through while keeping enough moisture for the plant.

### Is laterite soil good for tea?
Yes. Laterite soil is naturally acidic and found in hot, wet hill regions. It is low in fertility on its own, so growers add manure and fertiliser, after which it supports tea, coffee and other plantation crops.

### What climate does tea need?
A warm and humid climate with temperatures of about 20 to 30°C, rainfall of about 150 to 300 cm spread through the year, and no frost.

### Can tea grow in clay soil?
Not well. Heavy clay holds water and stays waterlogged, which damages tea roots. If clay is all you have, add plenty of organic matter and plant on raised beds or slopes.

## The bottom line

Tea needs acidic soil (pH 4.5 to 5.5), good depth, lots of humus, and above all good drainage. Laterite and loamy soils on hill slopes are the textbook answer, with alluvial terraces doing the job in Assam. Add a warm, wet, frost free climate and steady care of the soil, and you have the conditions behind every great cup of tea, from Assam to Kenya.
